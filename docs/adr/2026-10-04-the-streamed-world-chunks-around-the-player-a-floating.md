# 2026-10-04. The streamed world: chunks around the player, a floating origin, wind in the vertex shader
Status: accepted
Summary: A procgen scene bakes 48 m chunks on worker threads into three merged meshes each, uploads only what changed, measures everything from a nearby origin, and sways plants in the vertex shader.

## Context
The generator ([ADR: an endless world]) can answer for any point, but the engine draws what a scene lists, once, from meshes built at start. A walkable endless world needs four things the renderer lacked: meshes that come and go while the player moves, plants that are cheap enough to be tens of thousands, ground and trees the simulation can stand on and bump into, and numbers that stay accurate hundreds of kilometres from the origin (an f32 has seven digits).

## Decision
- **A `procgen` scene block** (`{ "seed", "relief", "trees", "flowers", "grass" }`) switches the world on. It is the ground everywhere: `GroundCandidates` asks the generator for the height (`terrain_height_at`), and `sim::player` adds the trunks within a couple of metres to the colliders each tick (`ProcgenGround`, a small cache of the last chunks' trunks), so the single-player game, the server and a client's prediction agree on every tree.
- **Chunks** (`procgen::chunk`): each 48 m chunk is baked into three merged meshes, ground (a 2 m grid whose heights, normals and colours come from the generator, so neighbours meet exactly), solid plants (trees and shrubs, which cast shadows) and flora (flowers and grass, which do not). A forest is a few hundred draw calls, not tens of thousands. Level of detail by distance (near, mid, far) caps the small plants per chunk and keeps the lowest-ranked ones, so thinner chunks are subsets of fuller ones and nothing pops; mid and far chunks use cheaper tree models; the farthest have no flowers (the ground carries a tint of their colour). Budgets are tests: a heavy near chunk under 110k triangles, the whole 270 m view about 1.8M triangles and 100 MB.
- **Streaming** (`procgen::stream`): `Streamer::update(eye)` works out the wanted chunks (nearest first), hands them to worker threads, and returns a delta (chunks to add or replace, chunks to drop); a margin stops a viewer on a detail boundary from rebuilding the same chunk back and forth. `StreamLayer` (`stream_gpu`) uploads a delta and draws, with its own object-uniform buffer (a chunk never moves), culling each chunk against the camera's and the light's frustum. The live renderer streams every frame; the offline `Renderer` (`frame`, tests) fills the view first, so a still frame is complete and deterministic.
- **A floating origin** (`render::render_origin`): once the camera is 4 km from the origin, camera, lights, objects, held items and chunks are all measured from the nearest multiple of 2 km, so the GPU never sees large numbers (tested at 400 km). Within 4 km it is zero and every existing scene renders exactly as before.
- **Wind** is in the vertex shader. A mesh vertex has a `sway` weight (metres moved in a full gust, larger for taller plants and for the free end of a stem or crown) and `wind_offset` moves it along a wave travelling across the field under a slow gust envelope, in the main and shadow passes. The scene time reaches the shaders even without a `clock`.

## Consequences
Easier: a game is `{"procgen": {...}, "clock": {...}}` plus rules; the world costs nothing until looked at, is identical on every machine, and needs no terrain on the wire. Harder: ground and trees exist only as queries, so lint/reach/walk analysis does not see them (they see no floor in a `procgen` scene) and any new tool must ask `ProcgenGround`; a `Vertex` grew a field (all other meshes carry 0); the first frames after a teleport show only what has streamed in (the live client fills nearest-first). Open: far trees are still real geometry (impostors would cut the cost in half), the horizon is hidden by haze rather than by a far-distance mesh, and the sway pattern shifts when the origin moves (a few centimetres, once per 2 km).
